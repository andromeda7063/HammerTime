=== SLIDE 02 ===
  Problem statement
  HammerTime 100  ·  VIT Chennai
  2
  When a car follows another, how much time does it lose, and where on the lap?
  Our output: a delay estimate for every driver in every mini-sector, relative to clean air.
  1
  Many factors at once
  Gap, tyre condition, driver pace, track position and interruptions all change lap time.
  2
  Telemetry hides the cause
  Proximity is observable; the aerodynamic loss is not directly measured.
  3
  Cars interact
  Treating each car as a separate time series ignores who is ahead of whom.

=== SLIDE 03 ===
  Isolated cars vs traffic graph
  HammerTime 100  ·  VIT Chennai
  3
  Typical approach
  Each car on its own
  One car's history only
  No neighbours, no edges
  XGBoost and LSTM baselines
  Cannot see who is ahead
  HammerTime
  All cars as a changing graph
  Directed leader to follower edges
  Edge weight falls with the gap
  Attention over nearby cars
  History window plus forecast

=== SLIDE 08 ===
  How it works: the traffic graph
  HammerTime 100  ·  VIT Chennai
  8
  20
  driver slots per snapshot
  ≤ 2 s
  gap needed for an edge
  σ = 1 s
  RBF width for edge weight
  Weight = exp(−gap² / 2σ²): 0.5 s → 0.88, 1 s → 0.61, 2 s → 0.14
  Node features: speed, throttle, five tyre-compound flags, tyre age, mini-sector. Inactive, pit and invalid cars stay masked.

=== SLIDE 14 ===
  Why model cars as a graph
  HammerTime 100  ·  VIT Chennai
  14
  30.5%
  lower MAE than XGBoost
  0.1%
  lower MAE than LSTM
  Only one
  that sees neighbouring cars
  Attention weights nearby leaders by proximity, so close cars count more
  Two layers pass information along a chain of cars in traffic
  Edges change with the gap at every timestamp
  Honest read: the margin over LSTM is tiny and R² is negative, so the graph's value is not yet proven.
